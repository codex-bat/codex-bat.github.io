The end is never the beginning

⠀ ⠀ ⠀In 1974, Hot-Topic was founded in New Hampshire, United States. It became a pinacle of the newspaper and magazine industry. It was the one-stop shop for all things news and media for America. The people behind Hot-Topic were always first to cover the most intriguing stories, and they had a knack for finding the most captivating angles.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀The news industry was quite competitive and by 1978, it started to fall flat on its head. A competitor of "Hot-Topic" had risen by this point, named "The Daily Massa", which was founded in 1976 in Boston, Massachusetts. The Daily Massa was a small newspaper that focused on local news and events --- it had a unique, british style, but by no means was it considered "small". It was run by an already well-established can factory company called "Thermann Company Ltd.". However, because of its british voicing, it gained popularity and started to take away Hot-Topic's readership. By 1981, The Daily Massa had become the leading newspaper in New Hampshire, and Hot-Topic was struggling to keep up.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀Hot-Topic was on the verge of being bought out by The Daily Massa, when a striking idea hit them. The nation-wide spread of The Daily Massa hurt its readership based in Boston, which left a gap in the market open to exploration that they have become blind-sighted to. Hot-Topic set up a new branch in Massachusetts, hitting the ground running with a new team of journalists and editors. They pulled the rug right out from the underneath The Daily Massa as they adopted Boston's local news and the people's respect. The rivalry became known as the "Role-switchers' war", because of both companies targetting the audience and market of the other. Rather Sadly, by 1984, the rivalry was all, but forgotten, as both newspaper outlets had started silencing any newspapers talking about it and had even localized their company registry. Both companies were fighting tooth and nail to obfuscate their origins, because it would hurt their public image if it became known that they weren't local. This way, Hot-Topic and The Daily Massa could continue to operate in their respective markets without any interference, effectively gatekeeping the news' industry to any new entrants.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀In 1985, Hot-Topic had already stabilized and was running smoothly. This led to the company expanding its readership to other states, again. Consequently, Thermann Company Ltd. started liquidizing some of its assets to focus on other ventures, before realizing their mistake. For a breif three months, they had lost their foothold in the newspaper and magazine industry and compensated by buying out other, small (but significant) newspaper companies in the United States. Before the end of 1985, Thermann Company Ltd. ended up forming a conglomerate named "The Thermann-Massa Group", which included The Daily Massa, but also their can factory, which they have yet to sell. This conglomerate became a major opponent in the industry and had a significant influence on the media landscape in the States. Legally, they were a powerful entity while Hot-Topic still remained a private company, but they were still a major competitor in the industry and continued to produce high-quality journalism that was respected by readers and peers alike.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀This conglomerate, however, was just the first step to a better plan, that would soon overtake the Hot-Topic group. The "Thermann-Massa" conglomerate had a vision to create a media empire that would dominate the industry and shape public opinion, but it all started with a simple, yet dirty, tactic: they founded a new newspaper outler named "Hotter-Topic", which made rounds as it began pumping out the freshest news. Journalists and editors would be flown across States just to be sent to the locations of interesting events and they'd cover everything live, writing it all on the hush-hush and sending it to the Thermann Press through private links. The name also helped confuse readers who didn't know the difference between the two publishers, which reeled in more readers. To use this momentum, The Thermann-Massa Group started acquiring more media outlets, including television stations (which were still risky at the time), radio stations, and online platforms. They would become a media powerhouse, with a vast network of publications and broadcasting channels under their control. In 1987, Hot-Topic would file a lawsuit against the Thermanns, but it was dismissed, because the law was yet to be made for these cases and it wouldn't be until 1996 --- nearly a decade later --- that the "Rights of Media Act" would be passed, which included the "Thermann clause", prohibiting media conglomerates from acquiring more than 10% of the market share in any given state. Furthermore, it would also prohibit the acquisition of a name similar to that of an already-existing competitor in any given media-related industry. Regardless of the later facts, Hot-Topic would start massive lay-offs as this one-sided lawsuit would lead them to their grave. Hot-Topic's Press body would also end up separated from the rest of the company and The Thermann's would take advantage of this and buy it out. This meant that Hot-Topic was decentralized and they didn't benefit from it. Hot-Topic's news would be going through a third-party pipeline, leaking information before it even hit the public. Because of this and the lawsuit, The Thermann-Massa Group would end up buying out Hot-Topic in 1990. This meant that it was now the sole powerhouse of all of the media in the United States. It became a monopoly that couldn't be challenged.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀What's most interesting, is that after all of this warfare, The Thermann-Massa Group would eventually be bought out by a foreign company in 1995. The circumstances around this event are still unclear and only speculations can be read, however what is known is that this company, named "The Global Media Group", was a collection of meticulously-acquired media outlets worldwide. And because of the local laws in whatever country it was founded in, its origin was unknown and there was no available roadmap of their hierarchy. As The Thermann-Massa Group slowly adopted the new changes that came with this acquisition, they would pitch an idea to the global body through their limited "support" pipeline. They suggested using their own format for archiving data (including media) and aptly named it "HTML", which stood for "Hot-Topic Mailing Luxury" --- a nudge to their past, which the global group would end up accepting through many pitches and negotiations. This format would be used to send and store any and all information within The Global Media Group's network and in 1997, they'd release it to the public as a service. The HTML service would become widespread with many public figures using it.

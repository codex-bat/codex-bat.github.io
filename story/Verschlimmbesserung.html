The meeting

⠀ ⠀ ⠀The smoke reaches even Wannsee; the windows are fogged by breath and tobacco. There's a cognac glass and it left a wet ring on the mahogany. I watch it spread. It feels like a mirror of the countless thoughts and screams I have, restrained in me. There is no hiding from Berlin's stench---dare I say, I missed it. My return from Moscow is three days old, and I am already exhausted---not from the journey, but from this room. No wonder, keeping all of this in is exhausting work---even for a general. OKH forced my attendance over Heydrich's protest. They need the Jews for labour; I need them for ditches. But these cretins see arithmetic as heresy. If only I were back on the front, I could at least keep my own men alive. Instead, I am stuck here, negotiating with bookkeepers who have never felt frostbite. I recognized all of the faces---I was trained on these men before this. Except, now that I look around... two chairmen stand out. They seem different. They're whole-blooded Germans---there are too few Prussians for comfort. These outright Germans tend to be more radical.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"... The Führer's order transcends logistics. The Jew is a biological poison. You do not negotiate with poison," says one icy tone, trying to act as the right-hand of the devil himself. One single Heydrich is all it takes to keep a room quiet.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"I don't believe this is the German way to go about this 'Final Solution', as you put it." says calmly, Friedrich---the representant of the Reich Chancellery. A bookkeeper.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"Actually," says one quieter Stuckart, "despite not agreeing on the legal basis for the deportation, I believe mandatory sterilization is preferable, because it preserves the spirit of the Nuremberg Laws." He believes himself to be the moderate voice. He is not.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"What you should be pressed about is the supply lines. I have just returned from Vyazma. Our Panzer divisions are stalled not because of the Red Army—but because the partisans blow up every rail bridge, and the partisans are fed by peasants whom we have starved and whose children we have shot." Finally, I get to voice our concerns. I'm tired of these men. I wish I could just go back to the East and tell my men they'll get to see their wives again by third sundown. "You haven't learned a thing from Moscow. You will do it again. You will push them to a river they cannot hold, in a winter they cannot survive, because you refuse to listen to men who have felt the frost." These elitist bureaucrats don't have any idea of the reality surrounding them so viciously. The room falls silent. The silence is far too long for this group of people, but it gets finally broken.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"I thought Mr. Keitel of the Wilhelm family said you'd be a mere observer." A diplomatic arrogance can be felt in Schöngarth's tone as he keeps his attentive eyes on me, "How did he put it? A liaison. Yes, that's the word he used." The silence decides to revisit us. The only sound is the scratch of Eichmann's fountain pen. He is taking notes. He is always taking notes. Outside, a tram rattles past on the Kurfürstendamm. Berlin is going about its day. Berlin has no idea. But...

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"I won't stand for your ignorance. I am here today only because your ideologies require my men to bleed for your paperwork." the room...

⠀ ⠀ ⠀is starting to pick up. "You dare question the Führer's orders?" asks Adolf Eichmann and simultaneously, you hear Heydrich's leather gloves creak as he clenches his fists. His knuckles snugly pressing against his palms. His tireless years of servitude towards impossibly inflated ideologies all shoved into that tight space behind his back.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"A touching speech, Colonel. But the Führer has decided. The war against the Jews is not subject to your quartermaster's arithmetic. You may return to your frozen front. We have work here." Infuriatingly ice cold. Indifferent.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀I scoff. "Return? I have just arrived, Heydrich. And I will not leave until you hear at least this and this goes for all of SS--- The Wehrmacht High Command despises you for all of your commands. Each and every command we get from you is seeded with apathy and carelessness. You will become our demise. We commend you for your ruthless efficiency and efficacy, however you're being limited, by this so-called 'hidden enemy' in our society." I have momentum. Why? Why do they let me speak?

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"I---"

⠀ ⠀ ⠀Kritzinger, with a small resemblance of respect left in this room for me, addresses me. He back-pedals. "Herr General, you speak of preserving these people? They are Jews. Their existence is a cancer on the Reich."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀I slam my palm on the mahogany table, rattling the cognac glasses. "Preserve?" I meet their eyes---one by one---and find nothing behind them but the arithmetic of genocide. I feel pure, unfiltered anger---the rage of a million dying soldiers. I see military suits that know nothing about us---the military. "You think I care about preserving them? I care about preserving my soldiers. You, Herr Staatssekretär, sit in your heated office and decree the deportation of 11,000 Jews from Warsaw---11,000 pairs of hands that, as we speak, are not digging anti-tank ditches outside Rzhev. Because of your racial paperwork, my Pioneers are digging frozen earth with their bare hands. Their fingernails are coming off in the frost. Do you know what that does to morale? Do you know what it does to a battalion when they see the SS shooting the very laborers who could be building their bunkers?"

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"You would seriously bring up morale as a justification for going against the Highest Command?" Otto Hofmann, you worm. I grip my teeth. I look at his wrinkled face. If only he had died sooner, it'd be one less big mouth I'd have to deal with. "You Wehrmacht officers care more about your supply trucks than the purity of German blood---you proved it, yourself just earlier. That is why we have the SS---to do what you are too weak to do." This is outrageous. This is---

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"Outrageous!" I roar, my voice cracking with frostbite. "Will you shut up and listen? The men ask why we fight like devils while wearing crosses---. That is a bullet I cannot stop with a field order. Herr Obergruppenführer, you and your army take away our chaplains just to replace them with your indoctrinated German Christians. You arrested Bernhard Lichtenberg. You think any of this will help us keep France?" A small silence. I take the opportunity to keep my stance---and my head---, while I still have it. "You are not strategists. You are bookkeepers. And bookkeepers will lose this war. Because when the snow comes again---and it will come again---my Sixth Army will be stretched across the Volga like a frozen carcass, and you will be here, in this warm room, tallying up the numbers of how many Jews you shipped east, while my men freeze to death because you had no compassion---not for the Jews, I don't ask for that---but for German bone and German steel. You treat the German soldier as expendable as you treat the Jew. That is not ideology. That is idiocy."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀Now the truly deafening long silence settles on the room. The coal smoke hangs between us. Coal smoke from where? Must be the bonfire. I hadn't picked up on it until just now. I can taste it. Bitter. Like the frost at Vyazma. No one touches their cognac. It sits there, warming, untouched---until Kritzinger circles his cognac's rim, sighing as he does so, breaking the dread of silence. "The General is crass, but his point is valid. We are the German state, not a roaming band of Freikorps. When the SS acts without the Army's coordination, we fracture the chain of command. That is not strategy; that is administrative suicide. If the Army falls in the East, who will guard the borders of the Reich? Your death's-head battalions? They are policemen, not soldiers. Von Hagen is right---you are gambling the entire Eastern Front for a few thousand early deportations. That is not efficiency. That is madness. I expect more from you Heydrich. Your bloodlust couldn't possibly blind you all this much."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀Heydrich does not answer Kritzinger. He stares at me instead. A long, measuring stare. Then he reaches into his jacket---slowly, deliberately---and pulls out a folded telegram. He slides it across the table. It stops in front of my untouched cognac.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"Read it," he says. His voice is no longer icy. It is flat. Tired, almost.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀I unfold it. It is a directive from the Führer's Chancellery, dated two days prior. It orders the "immediate removal and neutralization of any Wehrmacht officer exhibiting defeatist tendencies on the Eastern Front." It names no one. It does not need to. The blank space is a loaded gun.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"You think I am the monster here, Herr General?" Heydrich leans back, fingers steepled. "I am the leash. Without me, Himmler would have you shot by sundown. Without me, the Führer would replace every general with a Party loyalist who has never seen a map. You raged against my 'ideologies'---but I am the only reason your men still have generals at all. I tolerate your arrogance because you are useful. The moment you are not---"

⠀ ⠀ ⠀He taps the telegram with one finger. "---you are gone. Not by my hand. By his. And he will not give you a speech. He will not let you rage. You will simply cease to exist, and your men will wake up under a new commander who will deport those Jews twice as fast, and your beloved Sixth Army will still freeze, because the Führer does not care about fingernails. He cares about victory. And victory requires sacrifice."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀He stands. He walks to the window, turning his back to me. An insult. A dismissal. "You are not a threat to me, Colonel. You are a threat to yourself. Your 'clarity' is a luxury I cannot afford. I play the game. You scream into the void. Now get out of my villa before I decide that your usefulness has expired."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀I look at Kritzinger. He is pale. His hand, once circling the cognac, has stopped. He looks at the telegram. Then at me. Then at Heydrich's back. And I see it in his eyes---not fear, not anger. Recognition. He has just understood that the state he serves does not merely kill its enemies. It eats its own servants, one bite at a time.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀I stand. My chair scrapes the floor. No one moves. No one speaks. The coal smoke still hangs between us---a cruel reminder of my place. As I reach the door, Heydrich's voice follows me, quiet, almost amused:

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"And Von Hagen? Send my regards to Vyazma. You will be back there by Tuesday."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀I turn around. I meet his back. He has not even turned to face me.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"So that is it? A 'luxury' you couldn't afford? You'll turn a blind eye to the problems that will cost you this war?"

⠀ ⠀ ⠀He does not move.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"Perhaps," I say, my voice quieter now, "if Germany had not become what it is, you would have listened to reason. The people made this villa, Heydrich. Not you. Not the Führer. The people. And we are sending their sons to the Eastern Front to fight for a lie."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀A long pause. Then Heydrich turns. His face is unreadable. But his eyes—his eyes are curious. Like a scientist observing a specimen that refuses to die.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"Careful, Colonel."

⠀ ⠀ ⠀"You have already threatened me," I say. "How much more will you do? Execute me. I will not burden myself with guilt over my men's fate when it is you putting them through that suffering." I tap my foot in defiance. Everyone in the room nervously clears their throat or busies themselves.

⠀ ⠀ ⠀He doesn't smile. He doesn't even look at me. Cold. "Execute you? No." He gestures with two fingers---a lazy, dismissive flick toward the door. I'm quite dumbfounded. I've never been treated with such lack of protocol and German military courtesy by my superiors before. Before him. "Der Sicherheitsmann. Escort him out."

"Hey, no. You cannot just silence me like this!" Two SS officers come around and pull me away. They start dragging me away from the main conference table and towards the only door in the room. "LISTEN TO ME. YOU'LL REGRET THIS---" I try screaming as I'm pulled away. One of the men jabs my side with his Luger. The world goes blank---
